The headline. Across every test on this page, the draw history is indistinguishable from a fair random process, and no strategy beats a randomly filled ticket by a margin that survives correcting for the number of strategies tried. This page exists to measure that claim properly rather than assert it — and to be re-run automatically as new draws arrive, in case that ever stops being true.

1. The data is not what the archive hands you

The published archive runs from 1968, but the game changed repeatedly: a 6/49-style pool until 2004, 6/34 with a strong number up to 10 from 2005, and the current 6/37 with strong 1–7 only from 2012. Pooling those eras models a machine that never existed.

Worse is how the older pipeline handled it: it filtered rows by ball value, dropping any draw containing a number above 37. That is not a format filter, it is a selection on the outcome — it deletes precisely the draws that contained high numbers and keeps the low ones, manufacturing a downward bias in the surviving frequency table. In the pre-2004 years it discarded 1,843 of 2,019 draws that way. This report filters by date instead, which is unbiased, and uses the 1,629 draws from 2012 onward.

Two columns deserve attention. Log loss is a proper scoring rule — it grades the full probability distribution a strategy assigns, not just its top six — and a uniform guess scores exactly ln(37) = 3.6109. It is shown only for the 7 strategies that emit genuine per-number inclusion probabilities; the rest produce ranks, gaps or indicator values, and normalising those into a distribution would be an arbitrary transform that says nothing about calibration (for all-negative scores it collapses to the uniform prior and would report exactly the baseline no matter how the numbers were ranked). Among the strategies where the number means something, none beats the uniform guess, which means none holds information about the next draw even in principle. Verdict applies Holm–Bonferroni across all 14 strategies, because the highest of 14 noisy numbers is high by construction.

Expected value of a Lotto ticket is negative regardless of which numbers are on it — roughly half of ticket revenue is returned as prizes. The one genuine lever is which numbers, not whether: prizes are split among winners, and players over-pick dates, so a ticket weighted toward 32–37 wins no more often but shares less when it does. That is a payout effect, not a prediction effect, and it is the only edge in this repository.

6. Method

  • Era filtering by date, never by outcome, so the frequency table stays unbiased.
  • Chronological ordering. The archive ships newest-first; consuming it as-is trains a model to predict the past from the future.
  • Set-valued targets. Balls are published in ascending order, so per-position accuracy mostly measures the marginals of order statistics. Every metric here is order-invariant.
  • Walk-forward evaluation with periodic refitting; a strategy never sees a draw at or after the one it is predicting.
  • Exact null baselines (hypergeometric for matches, ln 37 for log loss) plus a Monte-Carlo null, and Holm–Bonferroni correction on every family of tests.
